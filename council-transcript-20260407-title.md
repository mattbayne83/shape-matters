# LLM Council Transcript — Section 5 Title & Subtitle
**Date:** 2026-04-07
**Topic:** Evaluating "Look Inside Your Structure" as the title for the Model Your Org section

---

## Framed Question

We're building "Shape Matters" (org-shape) — a physics-based organizational diagnostic tool. It's a single-page scroll site with 6 sections. By the time users reach the 5th section ("Model Your Org"), they've already been through:

1. **"Relays Destroy Signal"** — Bartlett's 1932 experiment showing info degrades per relay
2. **"The Telephone Effect"** — interactive relay simulator showing message distortion through layers
3. **"Three Disciplines, One Conclusion"** — Gemba Walk, Deming, Toyota evidence
4. **"Real Companies, Real Structures"** — 6 real companies compared (Valve to Amazon)

Section 5 is the interactive payoff — where users configure 5 sliders (Depth, Headcount, Fidelity/Layer, Cycle Time, Adaptability) and see three pillar scores (Fidelity, Latency, Agility) with expandable visualizations, What-If restructuring, and deeper metrics.

Current title: **"Look Inside Your Structure"**
Current subtitle: **"Depth, size, and culture create a signal profile unique to your org. This is what yours looks like."**
Section label (small caps above title): **"Model Your Org"**

Target audience: executives, org designers, consultants.

The question: Is this title + subtitle the strongest option for this specific position in the narrative arc? The user has already absorbed 4 sections of theory and evidence — this section needs to convert them from reader to participant. Does "Look Inside Your Structure" earn that transition? Does the subtitle set up the interactive experience well? What would be better?

For context, other titles that were considered and rejected: "See Your Organization's Shape", "Map Your Organization's Health", "How Much of Your Strategy Actually Lands?", "X-Ray Your Org", "What Gets Lost Between You and the Front Line?", "Your Org Obeys Physics", "Diagnose Your Organization", "What Shape Are You?"

---

## Advisor Responses

### The Contrarian

"Look Inside Your Structure" is passive. It's a museum placard, not a trigger. After four sections of escalating tension — signal degrades, relays destroy information, real companies prove it — you hand them "look inside"? That's a deceleration at the exact moment you need ignition.

The fatal flaw: "Look Inside" implies observation. You need them to **act**. They've been watching for four sections. This is where the frame shifts from "interesting theory" to "this is about YOUR org, right now." The title needs to create that confrontation.

The subtitle is worse. "Depth, size, and culture create a signal profile unique to your org" is explanatory text masquerading as a hook. It explains the mechanism before they've felt the consequence. An executive skimming this section header learns nothing urgent. There's no stakes, no tension, no implied question they need answered.

Look at what you rejected: "How Much of Your Strategy Actually Lands?" — that's the one. It takes everything from the previous four sections and aims it directly at the reader's ego. Every executive believes their strategy is landing. This title says "prove it." It converts the theoretical evidence into a personal challenge. The rejected list contains your best option.

For the subtitle, stop explaining the model. Instead, set up the interaction: "Configure your org's real structure. See what survives the layers." That's a dare wrapped in a tool description.

The section label "Model Your Org" is fine — it's functional wayfinding. But the title has to do emotional work the label can't. It needs to make them feel slightly uncomfortable, slightly curious, and slightly competitive. "Look Inside Your Structure" does none of those things.

One more problem: "signal profile" in the subtitle is jargon you invented. Nobody walks in knowing what that means. Don't introduce new vocabulary at the moment you need maximum clarity.

---

### The First Principles Thinker

The real problem isn't the title. It's the transition mechanics.

You've spent four sections building a case that organizational structure is governed by predictable forces — signal degradation, latency, rigidity. The user now believes this intellectually. The job of Section 5 isn't to label what they're about to do ("Look Inside Your Structure") — it's to make them feel the gap between what they now know and what they don't yet know about their own organization.

"Look Inside Your Structure" is passive. It describes the tool. It doesn't create tension. After four sections of mounting evidence that most orgs are bleeding signal, the user should feel a pull: *how bad is mine?*

The subtitle compounds the problem. "This is what yours looks like" promises a reveal before they've done anything. It's also descriptive rather than provocative — it tells them what will happen instead of making them want it to happen.

Strip it back to first principles: what converts a reader into a participant? Not an invitation. A provocation. The user needs to feel that *not* engaging the sliders means staying ignorant about something they now care about.

**Title:** "How Much Signal Survives Your Org?"

This works because it's a direct callback to Section 1 (signal degradation), it implies a specific quantifiable answer they don't yet have, and it frames interaction as the only way to find out. It's a question they can't answer without the tool.

**Subtitle:** "Set five parameters. See what your structure actually does to strategy, speed, and adaptability."

This is mechanical on purpose — it tells them the interaction is simple (five parameters) and the payoff is concrete (three outputs). No poetry. Just a clear transaction: input your reality, get your diagnosis.

The section label "Model Your Org" is fine. Small caps labels are functional, not persuasive. Don't overthink that one.

---

### The Expansionist

"Look Inside Your Structure" is underselling the moment. You've spent four sections building a case that organizational physics is real, measurable, and consequential. The user is primed. They're not here to "look inside" anything — they're here to discover something they suspect but have never been able to quantify.

The massive upside you're missing: this is the section where passive agreement becomes personal stakes. The right title doesn't just invite participation — it creates the sensation that the tool is about to reveal something the user already feels but couldn't articulate. That's the conversion moment consultants pay thousands for.

"Look Inside Your Structure" is clinical. It sounds like a diagram is coming. The subtitle compounds the problem — "signal profile unique to your org" is accurate but flat. It describes the tool instead of dramatizing the outcome.

Here's what I'd push toward:

**Title: "What Shape Are You?"**

Yes, it was on the rejected list, and that's a mistake. It's the strongest option by far. It's short, personal, slightly provocative, and it reframes the entire preceding narrative as building toward *this* question. Every section before it was about other organizations. Now it's about *you*. That pronoun shift is worth more than any clever metaphor.

**Subtitle: "Five inputs. Three scores. The physics of your organization — exposed."**

This does three things the current subtitle doesn't: it sets expectations for interactivity (five inputs), it promises a concrete deliverable (three scores), and it raises emotional stakes (exposed). The em dash creates a beat before the payoff.

The adjacent opportunity everyone will overlook: this title becomes the thing people say when they share it. "Have you done the 'What Shape Are You?' thing?" That's viral framing. "Look Inside Your Structure" is something nobody repeats to anyone, ever.

### The Outsider

"Look Inside Your Structure" is a dead phrase. It sounds like a tagline for an anatomy textbook. The problem is "look inside" — it's passive. You've spent four sections building momentum, showing people that their org is leaking signal, and now you're asking them to... look? That's a downshift.

The subtitle is worse. "Depth, size, and culture create a signal profile unique to your org" is explaining the tool instead of creating desire to use it. You're back in teacher mode right when you need to hand them the controls. A first-time visitor who scrolled through four sections of increasingly alarming evidence doesn't need another thesis statement. They need a prompt that makes inaction feel like a missed opportunity.

The rejected options tell me you've been circling the right energy without landing on it. "How Much of Your Strategy Actually Lands?" is the strongest of the bunch — it's a question that executives can't not answer. It makes the tool feel consequential rather than academic. But it was rejected, which makes me think the group is optimizing for cleverness over tension.

Here's what I'd do:

**Title:** "How Much Signal Survives Your Structure?"

This directly references the core concept they just learned (signal degradation), frames the tool as a personal reckoning, and uses "survives" — a word with stakes.

**Subtitle:** "Set five sliders. See what your org actually delivers."

Short. Action-oriented. "Actually" does the rhetorical work — it implies they don't currently know, which is the whole point.

The section label "Model Your Org" is fine. Small caps labels are furniture, not copy. Don't overthink it.

One more thing: every rejected title uses "your org" or "your organization." That's four words where two will do. "Your structure" is tighter and echoes the physics framing you've built.

---

### The Executor

"Look Inside Your Structure" is fine. It works. But "fine" isn't what you need at the transition from passive reading to active participation.

Here's the practical problem: "Look Inside" is still passive. The user has been "looking" for four sections. You need a verb that makes them DO something. The subtitle compounds this — "This is what yours looks like" promises a reveal, but they haven't configured anything yet. It's a lie until they touch the sliders.

What actually works at this point in the flow:

**Title: "Build Your Signal Profile"**

Three words. Active verb. "Build" means they're doing something right now. "Signal Profile" ties directly to the Bartlett/telephone content they just absorbed — it's not a new concept, it's the payoff of concepts they already understand.

**Subtitle: "Set five inputs. See what your structure actually does to information."**

This is an instruction, not a description. It tells them exactly what's about to happen (five sliders) and why they should care (connects back to the signal degradation they've been learning about).

The rejected options tell me you've been circling the right territory. "How Much of Your Strategy Actually Lands?" is the strongest rejected one — it's provocative and active — but it's a question when you need a command. "X-Ray Your Org" has the right energy but wrong metaphor (medical, not physics). "What Shape Are You?" is too cutesy for executives.

The section label "Model Your Org" already does heavy lifting. Let the title be the action and the subtitle be the instruction. Executives respond to directives, not invitations. Don't ask them to look. Tell them to build.

Monday morning move: swap the title and subtitle, test it with one real user, ship it.

## Chairman's Verdict

### Where the Council Agrees

"Look Inside Your Structure" is too passive for the climactic interactive moment. Every advisor independently reached this conclusion — the phrase decelerates when it should ignite. The current subtitle over-explains and introduces jargon ("signal profile") that hasn't been earned in the user's vocabulary.

The First Principles Thinker's "How Much Signal Survives Your Org?" was unanimously rated strongest. All five peer reviews converged on B. The reasoning is consistent: it callbacks to Section 1's Bartlett experiment, implies a quantifiable answer the user doesn't yet have, and frames the interaction as the only way to close that gap.

The subtitle's job is functional onboarding — reduce friction, preview the interaction mechanics, make the user reach for the sliders. It is not a second headline. It is not a persuasion surface.

### Where the Council Clashes

**Command vs. Question.** The Executor argues executives respond to directives ("Build Your Signal Profile"), not questions. The Contrarian and First Principles Thinker argue a question at this specific moment works because the user has been given four sections of evidence and now needs to feel the gap in their own self-knowledge. The question isn't weak — it's a dare.

The Executor has a point in general, but loses this specific argument. After four sections of mounting evidence, the user already feels implicated. A command ("Build") skips the emotional beat where they realize they don't know their own answer. The question creates a half-second of discomfort that makes the interaction feel necessary rather than optional.

**"Your Org" vs. "Your Structure."** The Outsider correctly notes "your structure" is tighter and echoes the physics framing. But the First Principles version uses "Your Org" which is warmer and more natural for executives who don't think of their company as a "structure." This is a genuine tension. "Structure" is more precise; "Org" is more human. For this audience, precision wins — they've been trained by four sections to think in structural terms.

### Blind Spots the Council Caught

The peer reviews surfaced three things no individual advisor caught:

1. **The subtitle should preview the slider labels.** Users arriving at Section 5 need to know what they're about to do, not why. "Depth, headcount, fidelity, cycle time, adaptability" — those words should appear in the subtitle so the interaction feels concrete before they touch anything.

2. **The user's emotional state is "implicated, seeking agency."** After four sections of evidence that flat orgs outperform, the user doesn't need more accusation. The tool is the reward. The title should offer agency through interaction, not pile on.

3. **The Expansionist's "What Shape Are You?" breaks the tool's own output.** The tool produces three pillar scores, not a shape classification. The title promises something the tool doesn't deliver. This would create a moment of confusion at the exact point you need trust.

### The Recommendation

**Title:** "How Much Signal Survives Your Structure?"

Takes the First Principles winner and swaps in the Outsider's tighter "Structure" over "Org." Callbacks to Section 1. Implies a number they don't have. Makes the interaction feel like the only way to answer.

**Subtitle:** "Set depth, headcount, fidelity, cycle time, and adaptability. See what your structure actually does to strategy."

Previews the five slider labels by name — eliminates the "what am I about to do" friction. "Actually does" implies their assumptions are probably wrong without saying so. Ends on "strategy" because that's what executives care about, not "information" or "signal."

**Section label:** Keep "Model Your Org" — it's wayfinding, not copy.

### The One Thing to Do First

Replace the current title and subtitle with the recommended text, then read Section 5 top-to-bottom in context with Sections 1-4 above it. The callback to "signal survives" should land as a narrative loop closing. If it doesn't — if there's a vocabulary mismatch between how Section 1 introduces signal degradation and how Section 5 references it — fix the echo in Section 1 first, not in Section 5.
